# How Digital Surveillance Actually Works

## The stuff that's confirmed

A lot of the devices in your house do collect data — that part is real and documented, not a theory.

- **Smart speakers** (Amazon Echo, Google Home) record short audio clips after they hear their "wake word." In 2019, reporters found that human contractors at Amazon and Google had listened to some of these clips to improve the software, including a few that were accidental activations. That was confirmed by the companies themselves, not a rumor.
- **Smart doorbells** like Ring have shared video with police departments through official partnerships — thousands of departments signed up between 2018 and 2021. Ring changed this policy in 2024 so police now have to get a warrant instead of just requesting footage directly.
- **Cars** built in the last ~15 years contain "event data recorders" (basically a black box) that log speed, braking, and seatbelt use in the seconds before a crash. This data can be pulled without your permission in most U.S. states, and it's used in accident investigations and court cases.
- **License plate readers** in the U.S. log roughly 1 billion+ scans a year combined across agencies and private companies, according to reporting based on public records requests. That builds a location history over time, even if no single scan seems significant.
- **Smartphones** track location through GPS, and apps with "background location" turned on can log where you go even when you're not using them. iOS and Android both let you check and turn this off per app — it's not automatic or hidden by default anymore, though it used to be less obvious.

## Real government programs, with real limits

- **PRISM** and related NSA programs were confirmed in 2013 when Edward Snowden leaked internal documents. These programs collected communications data, mainly targeting foreign communications, but sometimes picked up Americans' data too ("incidental collection") — this is documented in declassified reports, not speculation.
- **Section 702** of the FISA law lets the FBI search that collected data. Government transparency reports show the FBI ran millions of queries against this database in recent years (the number moves year to year and is publicly reported by the Director of National Intelligence).
- **Stingrays** (fake cell towers used by police) are real and have been used without warrants in some cities — Baltimore's use was documented through court records and ACLU lawsuits. Since then, several states have passed laws requiring a warrant for this.

**What's disputed here:** whether this level of collection is *necessary* for security or *goes too far* is a genuine, ongoing political and legal argument — courts, Congress, and privacy groups disagree. That's not something to flatten into "it's definitely fine" or "it's definitely a disaster."

## What companies actually do with your data

This part is less dramatic than it sounds but still worth knowing. Companies mostly use your data to predict what you'll buy or watch, not to build a psychological profile of your soul. Streaming services use what you watch to recommend more shows. Fitness apps sometimes sell aggregated (not usually name-attached) health data to advertisers or, in some cases, insurers — this has happened and been reported, but it's not universal to every app.

## Claims that are exaggerated or not well supported

A few common claims about surveillance go further than the evidence supports, and it's worth being precise here too:

- **"Quantum computers are decrypting your messages right now."** Not accurate. Quantum computers capable of breaking today's standard encryption don't exist yet. The real concern experts raise is "harvest now, decrypt later" — some agencies may be storing encrypted data now, hoping to decrypt it once quantum computers are good enough, which could be years away.
- **"There are zero ways to reduce your data footprint."** Not accurate. You can turn off location tracking per app, use messaging apps with end-to-end encryption (like Signal), and opt out of some data broker lists. None of this makes you invisible, but "zero options" overstates it.
- **"Your mattress knows your sex life and sends it to a server."** Some smart mattresses do track movement and heart rate for sleep-quality features, and that data goes to a cloud server for basic health tracking apps — sold in privacy policies as a feature, not hidden. Framing it as covert monitoring of intimate activity isn't what's actually documented.

## What you can actually do

- Check app permissions and turn off "always allow" location access for apps that don't need it
- Use a messaging app with end-to-end encryption for private conversations
- Turn off ad personalization in your phone's settings — it doesn't stop data collection but limits how it's used against you
- Know that none of this makes you untraceable, but it meaningfully reduces how much is collected by default

## The honest summary

Surveillance technology is real, well-documented, and has expanded a lot faster than most laws have caught up with — that's a fair thing to be concerned about. But some of the most viral claims about it (mind-reading algorithms, unavoidable total tracking, real-time quantum decryption) go further than the evidence. The accurate version is less cinematic than the internet's version, but it's the one you can actually act on.
