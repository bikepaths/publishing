The thesis itself is:

$$
\text{Silver purchasing power of food}
=
\frac{\text{silver value of 1964-standard coinage}}
{\text{current food price}}
$$

For $1 face value of 1964 90%-silver coinage:

$$
1\text{ silver unit}=0.7234\text{ troy oz fine silver}
$$

The hamburger test becomes:

$$
H_t=\frac{0.7234S_t}{P_t}
$$

where \(S_t\) is the dollar price of silver per troy ounce and \(P_t\) is the hamburger's dollar price.

For 1964:

$$
H_{1964}=\frac{0.15}{0.15}=6.67
$$

For 2026, the earlier estimate of 12 hamburgers implicitly assumes:

$$
0.7234S_{2026}\approx \$36
$$

and

$$
P_{2026}\approx \$3
$$

giving:

$$
H_{2026}\approx12
$$

Thus the proposition is not that “hamburgers experienced inflation” simply because their dollar price rose from $0.15 to approximately $3.00. Rather, the proposition is that:

$$
\boxed{\text{Hamburgers became cheaper in terms of silver}}
$$

if the actual 2026 ratio confirms that one 0.7234-oz silver unit buys more hamburgers than it did in 1964.

The broader thesis becomes substantially more interesting if we apply the same test to a basket of food:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Food price in silver}
=
\frac{\text{nominal food price}}
{\text{silver price in nominal dollars}}
}
$$

Then we can classify individual foods as:

* **Silver inflation:** more silver required per unit of food.
* **Silver deflation:** less silver required per unit of food.
* **Silver stability:** approximately the same silver required.

That would allow us to distinguish two phenomena that are normally conflated:

**Dollar inflation:** the price of food measured in Federal Reserve dollars.

**Silver inflation/deflation:** the price of food measured in a fixed quantity of monetary silver.

A particularly powerful analysis would use historical annual data from 1964 through 2026 for a standardized food basket—beef, chicken, eggs, milk, bread, flour, potatoes, sugar, coffee, etc.—and calculate:

$$
\text{Silver ounces per unit of food}
$$

year by year.

That would directly test whether the apparent enormous inflation in food prices since 1964 represents actual deterioration in food's purchasing power relative to silver, or primarily deterioration in the purchasing power of the Federal Reserve dollar.
